## Lecture Summary:

Academic collegiality is a moral obligation rooted in trust, respect, and shared commitment to research and teaching. Ethical life involves both mandatory compliance and aspirational striving for high standards. While professional codes educate and create accountability, they can't replace individual moral judgment. Academics must ask if they're becoming better professionals, not just following rules. 

Genuine support requires intellectual honesty, rigorous peer review, and fair resource sharing, while avoiding gossip, gatekeeping, or undermining juniors. Silence regarding misconduct is a violation; unethical behavior should be addressed directly or escalated to supervisors. Authorship must reflect actual contribution, avoiding guest, gift, or ghost statuses.

Power dynamics further complicate these ethics, as faculty influence over subordinates can lead to boundary erosion. Risks increase when roles conflict or power imbalances are significant. Research shows that discipline, career stage, gender, and culture influence ethical reasoning, making universal training ineffective. Institutionally, managerial forces can cause decollegialization by prioritizing administrative logic over academic values. Finally, educators serve as gatekeepers, balancing student mentorship with a duty to protect the public from incompetent trainees.
