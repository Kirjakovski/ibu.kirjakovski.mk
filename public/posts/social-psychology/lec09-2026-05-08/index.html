#09: Prosocial Behavior

SOCIAL PSYCHOLOGY
08 May 2026

Lecture Summary:

Why do people risk their lives for strangers? The Chernobyl liquidators waded into radioactive water knowing what it would do to them. Toddlers, less dramatically, hand toys to children they met five minutes ago. Both count as prosocial acts intended to benefit someone else. Altruism is the narrower category, reserved for cases where the helper takes on real personal cost.

Evolutionary psychology leans on kin selection (helping relatives propagates shared genes) and the norm of reciprocity (help given is help banked). Social exchange theory treats helping as a calculation: rewards like social approval or relief from one’s own distress, weighed against the costs of acting. Batson’s empathy-altruism hypothesis sits uneasily with the cost-benefit accounts. When empathic concern is strong enough, helping seems to become genuinely other-directed, and the data on this are harder to dismiss than economists usually want them to be.

Stable individual differences exist (the so-called altruistic personality), but situations carry most of the explanatory weight. Latané and Darley showed that the probability of any one person intervening drops as the number of witnesses rises, mostly through diffusion of responsibility. Milgram’s urban overload hypothesis adds that city dwellers learn to filter out social demands as a coping strategy for sensory excess. Cultural defaults can run the other way; in contexts organized around simpatía, low-cost helpfulness is a baseline expectation rather than an act of will.

Bystander intervention training exploits this. People who have been taught about diffusion of responsibility, pluralistic ignorance, and audience inhibition tend to spot the configuration when they’re in it, and they intervene at higher rates than untrained controls.
